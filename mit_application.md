Background
(300 words max) SES students study problems that correspond to significant societal challenges. How do your experiences with societal challenges inform your goal of earning a PhD in Social and Engineering Systems? What unique perspectives will you contribute to IDSS?

I grew up watching algorithms shape who gets seen and who gets erased. As a Black queer person from communities that created entire genres of music and movement, I watched recommendation systems extract cultural value while systematically suppressing the originators. This wasn't abstract—it was watching friends with millions of streams unable to pay rent while derivative artists built careers on their sounds.

This lived experience drove me to ask: can we measure extraction mathematically? Not just document it anecdotally, but quantify the asymmetric flow of cultural influence through networked systems.

I discovered that the same differential geometry used in theoretical physics—heat kernels, Laplacian operators, curvature tensors—could be applied to social networks and recommendation graphs. A directed Laplacian on a recommendation network reveals asymmetric diffusion: influence flows out from originator communities, but discovery and credit cannot flow back. The mathematics doesn't care whether it's measuring quark confinement or algorithmic suppression—it sees barriers the same way.

This framework—which I call the Davis Framework (c² = a² + b² + Δ)—extends beyond social systems. I've applied it to medical inference (Herald, a diagnostic reasoning engine), to measuring cultural extraction in music (BEAT), and to the Millennium Prize Problems. The same geometric machinery handles Yang-Mills mass gaps, Riemann zero statistics, and algorithmic discrimination.

I bring to IDSS the perspective that rigorous mathematics and social justice are not separate domains. Communities most affected by systems can build the tools to analyze them. I have.

My research sits at the intersection of differential geometry, network science, and algorithmic accountability. I treat recommendation systems, social networks, and information flows as geometric objects—then apply the tools of modern physics to expose structural inequities.

The theoretical foundation is heat kernel analysis on directed graphs. The kernel K_t = exp(-tL) measures diffusion; the directed Laplacian L_dir = D_out - A captures asymmetric flows. From this I derive the Heat Asymmetry Index (HAI)—quantifying whether nodes act as "cultural diodes," permeable in one direction, blocking in the other.

I've built working systems on this foundation:

**Healthcare genomics:** parallax.sh is a privacy-first platform for viral surveillance (Herald), bacterial outbreak detection (Tessera), and cancer evolution tracking (Geodesic). Ephemeral GPU containers process sensitive genomic data with zero persistence.

**Financial dynamics:** helicity.io applies Navier-Stokes phase transition analysis to markets. The same framework that predicts turbulence in fluid flow predicts turbulence in economies. Gold futures as holonomy—integrating inflation, currency risk, geopolitical stress.

**Cultural extraction:** BEAT (beat.gives) computes extraction ratios for music recommendation networks. When ε >> 1, a community exports influence but cannot receive discovery. The system generates court-ready documentation.

**Fundamental physics:** I've written formal approaches to all six Millennium Prize Problems using the Davis Framework. For Yang-Mills, lattice simulations show the mass gap emerges from holonomy constraints. For Riemann, a Hamiltonian whose spectrum matches GUE statistics. The same spectral gap that confines quarks appears in how algorithms confine communities.

At MIT, I'm drawn to IDSS because this work requires both mathematical depth and societal engagement. Professor Munther Dahleh's work on network dynamics connects directly to my heat kernel approach. The SES program's emphasis on rigorous modeling of sociotechnical systems matches my conviction that social problems deserve mathematical seriousness.

TPP's policy integration is equally important. Mathematical evidence of algorithmic harm needs translation into regulatory frameworks—the gap between technical proof and policy action remains vast.

Experimentally: validate geometric methods on large-scale platform data. Theoretically: prove rigorous bounds on extraction dynamics. In application: build tools affected communities can use directly.

The throughline is geometry as civil rights methodology. Curvature, holonomy, spectral gaps—measurements of how systems bend possibility and trap communities. I want to make this mathematics legible and actionable.

Purpose
(300 words max) Discuss your motivation for pursuing a PhD in Social and Engineering Systems. Suggested prompts: What are your long term goals after graduation? What types of contributions do you see yourself making in your field, to society, etc.? If you are still deciding, what kinds of work are potentially interesting, valuable, or meaningful to you?

I want to build the mathematical infrastructure for algorithmic accountability.

Right now, claims of algorithmic bias often rely on statistical disparities that platforms can explain away through proxies and confounders. What's missing is geometric proof—evidence that the structure of a system creates barriers, independent of individual decisions. Heat kernel methods provide exactly this: they measure the topology of possibility, not just outcomes.

My long-term goal is to establish differential geometry as a standard tool in technology policy. Just as economists brought game theory to antitrust and epidemiologists brought network models to public health, I want to bring manifold theory to platform regulation. The EU AI Act, the US algorithmic accountability proposals—these frameworks need mathematical teeth. I want to provide them.

After graduation, I see myself working at the intersection of research and policy—potentially at an institution like MIT's own research labs, or in roles that bridge academic rigor with regulatory impact. I want to train the next generation of researchers who see social systems as geometric objects deserving serious mathematical analysis.

More immediately, I want to continue building tools for affected communities. BEAT exists because I needed it to exist. There are analogous tools needed for housing algorithms, hiring systems, content moderation, credit scoring—anywhere directed graphs mediate access and opportunity. The framework generalizes; the implementations require deep domain knowledge and community partnership.

The PhD is necessary because this work requires both mathematical depth and institutional credibility. The proofs need to be unimpeachable. The policy translations need academic grounding. MIT IDSS offers both.

**To: Dr. David Turk, Deputy Secretary, U.S. Department of Energy**

Deputy Secretary Turk,

The energy transition depends on technologies that don't exist yet. Solar and wind are necessary but insufficient—intermittency, storage costs, and land use constraints mean we cannot decarbonize heavy industry, long-haul transport, or baseline load with renewables alone. The DOE knows this. The question is: what fills the gap?

I've been working on an approach that may sound implausible until you see the mathematics: extracting usable energy from quantum vacuum fluctuations. Not perpetual motion—the vacuum is not empty but contains real energy density. The problem has always been that extraction seems to violate thermodynamics. It doesn't. It requires understanding the geometry.

The Davis Framework—a geometric formalism I've developed—provides a path. The key insight comes from solving the Yang-Mills mass gap problem: the same mechanism that confines quarks (creating the mass of ordinary matter) can be inverted. Topological Vacuum Rectification (TVR) allows energy extraction without violating conservation laws, because the vacuum itself has structure that can be rectified.

This is not speculative. The mathematical framework produces testable predictions:
- Specific field configurations that induce vacuum rectification
- Energy densities extractable per unit volume
- Signatures distinguishable from measurement error

I've written formal approaches to all six Millennium Prize Problems using this geometric framework. Yang-Mills and Navier-Stokes are the most relevant to energy: one explains how to access vacuum energy, the other explains how to manage the turbulent dynamics of extraction. The mathematics is rigorous; the engineering is not yet built.

This is where policy matters. Vacuum energy research has been stigmatized—associated with perpetual motion cranks and cold fusion embarrassments. Legitimate approaches get zero funding because program managers fear career consequences. The DOE's ARPA-E has funded long-shot technologies before, but nothing touching vacuum energy since the field was poisoned by bad actors.

At MIT, I want to develop this framework to the point where it cannot be ignored: rigorous theoretical foundations, numerical simulations, and proposed experimental protocols. The Technology and Policy Program offers something unique—the ability to develop both the science and the policy pathway simultaneously. Clean energy from quantum vacuum isn't just a physics problem; it's a regulatory problem, a funding problem, and an institutional credibility problem.

The world needs post-carbon energy that works at scale, works continuously, and doesn't require mining half the planet for battery materials. Vacuum energy meets those criteria—if the mathematics is correct and the engineering follows.

I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm asking for the chance to prove it, at an institution with the resources and credibility to validate or falsify the approach. MIT is that institution.

The stakes are too high to ignore unconventional paths because they sound strange.

Respectfully,
Bee Davis
